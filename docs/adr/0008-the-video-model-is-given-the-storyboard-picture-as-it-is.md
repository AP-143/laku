# The video model is given the storyboard picture as it is and decides everything else itself

Partly superseded by [0009](0009-storyboard-and-video-are-two-features-again.md): the planner no longer gets the scenes or the Seller's first request (the brief), only the picture and the words typed with the video request.

The Seller wants the video made from the storyboard, with the AI thinking for itself: its style, the text it shows, its effects, the camera, the acting and the sound are the video model's own choices. So every clip starts from the storyboard picture the Seller approved, unchanged, and the app gives the model almost no instructions.

Earlier versions did the opposite, in two ways. They drew a clean copy of the picture first (no text, labels or bubbles; about US$0.034) and drew the text in the edit with the app's own font and bubbles. Then they sent a long clip prompt of rules (a professional editor's camera moves, sound design, micro-expressions, something moving in every second, how the text and effects should look, what the sheet's frames are). The last real video made with that pile of rules came out as a mess, and the Seller ordered all of it removed. An even earlier real run had shown that a model that sees a storyboard sheet may copy it (strips of the sheet at the top and bottom, doubled text); that risk is accepted and only a real run shows whether it comes back. The next real run did come back with it: one clip played a whole scene of the picture, its frame, the words "storyboard" and "panel 9:16" and the label "4. Opening" included, and the closing call to buy never appeared. The causes were in our words, not in a missing rule: the picture prompt asked for a "storyboard sheet" in "9:16" made of "panels", the planner was shown numbered titles and panel wording and echoed them in the directions, and the clip prompt did not say the picture is a plan, and the caption words did not reach the directions.

- **The clip prompt is one short sentence of what the picture is and which part to play**: "The reference image is the storyboard of an ad: a plan of its scenes to film, not footage. Make the finished ad video from it. This clip plays: <direction>". What the picture is is information, not a rule. Nothing about style, text, effects, camera, acting, sound, motion, the art direction or the frames of the sheet. The art direction is not repeated either: it is already in the picture.
- **Two short sentences are added only when they apply, because the app adds that sound afterwards**: "The person does not speak (a narrator is added later)" when a voice-over was asked for (ADR 0005), and "No background music (it is added later)" when music was asked for (ADR 0007). Without them the model's own sound is left alone.
- **The planner only shares the story.** It is given the scenes by number only ("Scene 1: ..."), with no titles and no panel wording, and each clip's direction is one or two plain sentences: the story of that part told as a film scene (what happens, who is in it, what is shown), in order, none skipped. A scene's caption words are part of what is shown, copied as written, so the video model has them. It is told to say nothing about how it is filmed, edited, acted, heard or how the text looks. The planner still chooses what the app itself adds: the voice and the narration lines, the music brief, and the colour grade (ADR 0006).
- **Nothing is added to the price.** The video costs only its seconds (US$1 for 10 seconds), plus voice and music when asked.

## Considered options

- **Keep the clean copy and the app-drawn text.** Rejected by the Seller: it replaces the style of the storyboard with the app's.
- **Keep a long prompt of professional-editor rules.** Rejected by the Seller after a real result: the more we dictate, the worse the video.
- **A clean keyframe per clip.** Not chosen: the clip would see only its first scene.
- **Ask the video model for the whole video in one clip.** Not tried: a clip of 6 seconds is the longest proven on the real model.

## Consequences

- The tests check that every clip starts from the stored storyboard picture, that no image is drawn or booked for the video, that the clip prompt is short, holds the direction and the storyboard sentence, carries the silent-person and no-music sentences only when they apply, and holds none of the old rule words. Whether the result is good is only visible on a real run; if it is not, the clip prompt and the planner's direction are the first places to look, and the answer is probably to say less, not more.
- A browser that still sends a clean copy has it ignored.
- **The picture has no words about itself.** Its prompt asks for one tall picture of the ad's scenes in a grid and says positively that the only text on it is the number-and-title pills and the captions; it never says "storyboard", "sheet", "panel" or "9:16" (the shape is asked through the image call).
- **What the video model is told is logged.** The server prints `[clip n/m] prompt: ...` for every clip (the prompt only, never a key or an image), so a bad clip can be traced to its words.
- The first request of the Seller ("buatkan storyboard dan video 10 detik") is read by the storyboard planner, which adapts the scenes and the art direction to it, and the words themselves travel with the storyboard as the brief (not a summary written by a model: a first version let a model summarise it and a request for 10 seconds came back as a 20 second video). The video planner gets those words, and any typed with the video request, and is told to do what they ask; a length written in them is found by a fixed pattern and must be the length of the video: the planner is asked once more if it chose another, and a plan that is still the wrong length is fitted to the asked length in code. Only text-model calls are ever repeated, never a video call. The price question and the plan sent back with the yes are unchanged, so the brief cannot change what was priced.
- Still open: whether a single longer clip gives a better result (needs real runs).
